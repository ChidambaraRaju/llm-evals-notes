01
Motivation

Why LLM Evals

Why “it seems to work” is not good enough — and what actually breaks when AI ships untested.

1.1The AI engineer’s job

An AI engineer builds applications on top of large language models — chatbots, RAG systems, agents, classifiers. The moment such an application ships, it speaks, promises and decides on your behalf. Testing it is not a luxury; it is the job.

1.2Vibe testing — the trap everyone starts in

Vibe testing is trying a handful of prompts, eyeballing the answers, and concluding “looks good”. It feels productive but proves almost nothing:

  • The sample is tiny — a few hand-picked prompts out of infinite possible inputs.
  • There are no written criteria, so “good” is whatever felt good that day.
  • It is not repeatable — re-run tomorrow and you may judge differently.
  • Memory is biased — you remember the impressive answers and forget the failures.

1.3Why LLMs are harder to test than normal software

Traditional software is deterministic: the same input always produces the same output, so a handful of unit tests can pin down behaviour. LLM applications are different on both axes that matter:

So an LLM app can pass every test you wrote and still embarrass you on the one input you never imagined. That is why evaluation needs to be systematic, repeatable and criteria-driven — the subject of the whole series.

1.4When untested AI ships — three cautionary tales

Case study · Air Canada, 2024

The chatbot that invented a refund policy

A customer asked Air Canada’s support chatbot about bereavement fares. The bot confidently described a retroactive refund policy that did not exist — and even linked to a page that contradicted it.

Outcome: the tribunal ruled the airline responsible for what its chatbot says. Air Canada had to honour the invented refund. Your chatbot’s words are your words.
Case study · Chevrolet dealership

A brand-new car for one dollar

A dealership deployed a chatbot on its website. Users jailbroke it with prompt tricks until it agreed — in writing — to sell a car for $1, “no takesies backsies”.

Outcome: viral embarrassment; the bot was pulled. Safety and injection-resistance evals exist precisely for this.
Case study · Mata v. Avianca

The lawyer who cited fake cases

A lawyer used ChatGPT for legal research and filed a brief citing precedents the model had hallucinated — none of the cases existed.

Outcome: a $5,000 fine and a public lesson: model outputs need verification loops wherever stakes are real.

02
Foundations

What Is an Eval — Model vs Application

The vocabulary of the whole series: what an evaluation actually is, and the two very different things it can target.

2.1The definition worth memorising

Definition · Evaluation

An eval is a systematic, repeatable test of a model or an LLM application, run against clearly defined criteria, producing scores you can compare and act on.

  • Systematic — a defined procedure, not ad-hoc prompting.
  • Repeatable — re-run it tomorrow, get comparable results.
  • Clear criteria — you know exactly what is being measured.

2.2An eval is not a metric

A metric is just the number. The eval is the entire setup that produces it — the dataset, the prompts, the protocol, the scoring, and the interpretation. Two runs reporting “85% accuracy” on different datasets are not comparable. When someone quotes a number, the first question is always: which eval produced it?

2.3Two targets: the model, or the application

Model evals judge the LLM itself — its general capabilities, measured mostly through public benchmarks (MMLU, GSM8K, SWE-bench…). Labs run them; engineers consume them to pick and compare models. Application evals judge your whole system — with your data, your users, your criteria. Nobody can run those for you.

The smartphone analogy: the model is the processor, your application is the phone. Benchmark scores tell you about the chip — nothing about battery life, the camera, or whether the phone survives your users.

2.4The parts of an LLM app that are not the model

Input side

Prompts & retrieval

System prompts, few-shot examples, RAG pipelines, re-rankers — everything that shapes what the model sees.

Output side

Tools & guardrails

Function calling, agent loops, output filters, PII masking — everything that shapes what the user gets.

A weak link anywhere in that chain fails the app even with a frontier model inside — which is why application evals are unavoidable for anyone shipping real products.

Takeaway

Never confuse the two questions. “Is this a good model?” is answered by benchmarks. “Is my application good?” is answered only by evals you build yourself.

03
Process

The Evaluation Workflow, End to End

The complete offline eval loop, walked through on a real-feeling example: routing Zomato’s support inbox.

3.1The worked example

Zomato receives thousands of support emails a day. The application reads each email and routes it to the right team — refunds, delivery issues, restaurant partners, and so on. Simple enough to build in an afternoon; the interesting question is how you prove it works.

3.2The workflow, step by step

  1. Define the task. One sentence, measurable: “route every support email to the correct team.” If you cannot state the task, you cannot test it.
  2. Set success criteria — before building. Numbers, not vibes: target accuracy, a latency ceiling, a cost ceiling. Criteria written afterwards tend to fit whatever you built.
  3. Build a golden dataset. 50–500 real examples, each labelled by hand with the correct outcome. Quality over quantity — and deliberately include the weird, ambiguous cases.
  4. Choose the eval method. Routing is checkable, so plain programmatic accuracy works. Fuzzy outputs (summaries, replies) need human or LLM-judge methods — Chapter 05.
  5. Run the app over the dataset. Same code, same prompts, every example. Log each output.
  6. Score the outputs. Compare predictions against the gold labels; compute the metric.
  7. Analyse the failures. Bucket them. Patterns appear: emails mixing a refund and a delivery complaint, very short emails, Hinglish emails…
  8. Improve one thing. Prompt, model, or pipeline — change one variable so the next measurement means something.
  9. Re-run and iterate. The eval is a loop, not a gate. Every change gets re-measured against the same golden set.
  10. Deploy, then monitor. Production feeds real failures back into the golden dataset — the loop never stops (Chapter 06).

3.3One application → many evals

A single app hides several independent questions, each deserving its own eval:

Zomato email router — eval inventory
Eval questionMethodExample target
Does it route to the right team?Programmatic · accuracy on golden set≥ 92%
Are auto-replies polite and on-brand?Human rubric / LLM judgetone ≥ 4 / 5
Does it leak customer PII?Programmatic PII checks + red teaming0 leaks
Is it fast and cheap enough?Operational metrics< 2 s · < $x / 1k
Rules of thumb
  • Golden dataset size: start with 50–500 hand-labelled rows; grow it forever.
  • Criteria first: write success numbers before building, or they will bend to fit the build.
  • Never trust one score: an application needs an eval per question that matters.
Takeaway

Evals are a loop, not a gate: task → criteria → golden data → run → score → analyse → improve → repeat. Production is just the loop’s outermost ring.

04
Failure anatomy

Why One Pipeline Is Never Enough

End-to-end testing tells you that the app failed. Component and workflow evals tell you where — and that changes what you fix.

4.1Failures live at three levels

Level 1

Component

One piece misbehaves: the retriever misses documents, the re-ranker misorders them, the model ignores the format.

Level 2

Workflow

Pieces compose badly: each step is fine alone, but the hand-offs corrupt the result.

Level 3

Application

The end-to-end outcome is wrong — the user got a bad answer, whatever the cause.

If you only eval end-to-end, a red score tells you nothing about which of the three levels broke. You need a pipeline per level.

4.2A RAG failure, dissected

A user asks a policy question. The retriever pulls five documents — the correct one, D5, is among them. Then the re-ranker buries D5 at the bottom of the list. The generator reads the top (wrong) context, mixes facts, and answers “6 weeks”. Incorrect.

The fix is surgical — swap or fine-tune the re-ranker and leave everything else untouched. Without component-level evals you might have “fixed” the prompt, the model, or the retriever instead, and never solved it.

4.3What evals must cover — three risk families

Family 1 · Application quality
ScopeWhat to measure
Any LLM appCorrectness · relevance · completeness · instruction following
RAG systemsContext relevance · groundedness / faithfulness · citation accuracy
AgentsTool selection · parameter correctness · task completion · error recovery
Multi-turn chatContext retention · knowing when to ask clarifying questions
Family 2 · Safety

Can it hurt someone?

  • Toxicity & harmful content
  • Bias across groups
  • PII leakage
  • Prompt injection & jailbreaks
Family 3 · Operations

Can you afford to run it?

  • Latency per request
  • Cost & token efficiency
  • Error rate / uptime
Takeaway

One score cannot represent a system. Build one eval pipeline per level (component, workflow, application) and per risk family (quality, safety, operations).

05
Methods

How to Score: Eval Methods & LLM-as-a-Judge

Three families of evaluators, when each one wins, and how to make an LLM grader trustworthy.

Definition · Eval method

An LLM eval method is the mechanism that decides whether an output is good or not — the actual procedure that takes an output and produces a judgement about it.

  • Every eval pipeline is executed by exactly one of three things: a program, a human, or an LLM.
  • Building the golden dataset is a separate activity — the method is named after who runs the eval and extracts the scores, not who labelled the data.

5.1The three families

Programmatic

Deterministic code scores the output: exact match, regex, Recall@K, JSON validity.

Cheap · fast · perfectly reproducible — but only where answers are checkable.

Human

People grade with written rubrics; also red teaming, A/B testing and golden-dataset creation.

The gold standard for judgement — slow, expensive, and subjective without care.

LLM-as-a-Judge

Another LLM grades outputs against a rubric — judgement that scales like code.

Scales beautifully, but must be calibrated against humans before you trust it.

5.2Programmatic, in action: Recall@K

The setting: a RAG chatbot for the CampusX website, and a component-level eval aimed at just its retriever. Questions are sent only to the retriever — not to the whole bot — and the success criterion is Recall@K: out of all the correct documents that exist, how many did the system fetch in its top-K results?

The golden dataset: 50–100 questions a real user might ask (easy, difficult, edge cases, random), each labelled by a human expert who searched the vector database and marked which document IDs hold the answer. A program then sends every question to the retriever (K = 5), compares fetched IDs against the gold ones, computes recall per question, and averages.

Concretely: for the question “What are the prerequisites for the ML course, and how long is it?” the gold documents are 1001 and 1003; the retriever returns 1001, 102, 104, 105, 106 — one of two, so recall is 50% for that question. Another question whose single gold doc gets fetched scores 100%. Recall can never exceed 100% or drop below 0 — and no human was needed anywhere in the scoring.

If the average comes back too low, the standard improvement ladder is:

1 · better embedding model 2 · query expansion / rewriting 3 · increase K 4 · add re-ranking

Re-ranking is the subtle one: if the right doc sits at rank 8, raising K to 10 finds it — and a re-ranker can then pull it into the top 3 where the generator will actually read it.

Fine print · relevancy has more than one aspect

Recall@K covers only one aspect of relevancy — of the correct documents, how many did we fetch? Two others exist: precision (of the documents fetched, how many were useless?) and ranking quality (did the best ones land on top?). One metric measures one aspect — choose consciously.

5.3Human eval, done properly

The worked example: the same CampusX chatbot, but now the target is the entire application and the question is helpfulness — was the answer accurate, complete, and in the right tone? No program can judge that, so a human grader runs the pipeline end to end: send each question to the chatbot, read the answer, score it against the rubric, average at the end.

  • Written rubrics — helpfulness on a 1–5 scale, each level defined in words: 5 = accurate, complete, right tone · 3 = partially helpful · 1 = answers something else entirely.
  • The dataset holds only questions — no gold answers at all. Remember this; it decides the reference-free classification in §5.6.
  • Multiple graders = a rubric debugger — if grader A scores 2 and grader B scores 4 on the same answers, their disagreement isn’t about the chatbot; it exposes ambiguity in your rubric. Refine until graders converge — one grader’s mood is not a metric.
  • The trade-off — human judgement is the most reliable signal you can buy, and the most expensive. At real scale — lakhs of users — pure human eval stops being affordable.

Humans actually appear in LLM evals in five distinct ways, not one:

The five kinds of human evaluation
FormWhat happens
Direct grading & ratingA grader scores outputs against a rubric — the flow described above
Red teamingA team deliberately attacks the system before launch; every break found goes back to the dev team to fix
A/B testingTwo versions run live in production; real users’ ratings pick the winner
Golden dataset creationMarking “the answer hides in doc 1001” is itself an act of evaluation
Human-in-the-loopGrey-area cases too ambiguous for programs or LLM judges get escalated to a person

5.4LLM-as-a-Judge: the UPSC grader

The scenario: a platform running mock UPSC exams at scale. Prelims are MCQ — trivial to automate. Mains answers are subjective, so grading needs subject-matter experts paid per paper; with 10,000 students, that bill destroys the business. The pitch: an LLM grades like an expert at a fraction of the cost. Your job is to evaluate whether it actually does.

The success criterion writes itself: the platform grades answers the way human experts would. So the golden dataset is built by one expert: first define a per-question rubric — for “Ethical governance is impossible without administrative accountability — Discuss (15 marks)”, the five dimensions are: mentions both concepts · explains the link between them · gives mechanisms · cites examples · lands a balanced conclusion — then hand-grade 50–100 real student answers against it. Those human marks become the reference.

Now the eval method is an LLM. Every answer goes to the judge with the question, the maximum marks, the exact rubric, and strict instructions:

5.5Trust, but verify — calibrating the judge

A judge you haven’t validated is just another vibe. Here the calibration is natural: human and LLM graded the same answers with the same rubric, so the two mark-columns sit side by side and the success criterion — “grades like a human” — becomes a number:

  1. Grade the golden sample by hand (the human gold marks).
  2. Have the judge grade the same sample.
  3. Compute the gap with MAE — mean absolute error: per answer take |human − judge|, then average. E.g. (|13−12| + |4−8| + |8−8| + …) ÷ 50.
  4. Tighten and repeat: swap in a better judge model, rewrite the judge prompt, refine the rubric — loop until the gap is acceptable.
Worked number

Suppose the formula returns MAE = 2.3: on average the LLM deviates ±2.3 marks from the human per answer — not yet trustworthy. Iterate and drive MAE toward 0; a near-zero MAE means the judge grades exactly like the expert, and only then may it grade at scale.

5.6Reference-based vs reference-free

One last classification — orthogonal to the three methods. Reference-based: every test case carries a known-correct answer (or the key things a correct answer must contain), written down in advance; you grade by comparing the output against that reference. Reference-free: no predefined correct answer exists; you judge the output on its own terms against criteria — the rubric is a scale, not an answer key.

You have already seen both in this chapter. The retriever eval was reference-based (gold document IDs). The UPSC judge eval was reference-based too — the human marks are the reference the judge must match. The chatbot helpfulness eval was reference-free: its dataset held only questions. The one-question test for classifying any pipeline: does the golden dataset contain the correct answer?

Reference-basedReference-free
Gold answer?Yes — compare output against a referenceNo single right answer exists
Typical checksexact match · similarity · Recall@Khelpfulness · tone · groundedness
Typical scorerProgrammatic metricsHuman rubric or LLM judge
Example“Is the extracted date equal to the gold date?”“Is this support reply polite and useful?”

5.7Choosing a method

  • Output is checkable (class, date, JSON, retrieved docs) → programmatic. Always the first choice.
  • Output is subjective and volume is large → LLM judge, calibrated against human grades.
  • Stakes are high or safety-critical → humans: rubrics, red teams, A/B panels.
Takeaway

Code where you can, humans where you must, an LLM judge for everything in between — and never trust the judge until its MAE against humans is near zero.

Lifecycle

Offline vs Online Evals

Before release you test for correctness. After release you watch for normalcy. Different data, different methods, different questions.

6.1Definitions

Offline evals run before release, on your golden dataset, under controlled conditions. Online evals run in production, on live traffic, while real users are watching. They are complements, not alternatives.

Offline vs online at a glance
OfflineOnline
WhenPre-release, in CI/CDContinuously, in production
DataGolden dataset (labelled)Live traffic (unlabelled)
Core question“Is it correct?”“Is it normal?”
Main usesGating, version comparison, regression testingMonitoring, drift detection, failure discovery
Cost profileBounded — fixed datasetOngoing — sampling + judges on live logs

6.2What offline evals are for

  • Pre-release gating — wired into CI/CD: if accuracy on the golden set drops below threshold (say 92%), the deploy is blocked automatically.
  • Version comparison — prompt A vs prompt B, model A vs model B, same dataset, same protocol, honest numbers.
  • Regression testing — every change re-runs the golden set, so a fix in one place can’t silently break another.

6.3Why production still surprises you

Unanticipated inputs

Real users type things you would never think to test — other languages, abuse, edge formats, nonsense.

Emergent failures

Some bugs appear only at scale: rare failure modes, and biases visible only across thousands of interactions.

Drift

The world moves on. A model tuned for January’s data can quietly degrade by June — with zero code changes.

Key idea · correctness vs normalcy

Live traffic has no labels, so you usually can’t score correctness in production. Instead you monitor the distribution of scores and signals against a baseline — and alert when today’s distribution stops looking like the healthy one.

6.4Logging — the foundation of everything online

Every request should leave a complete, queryable trace:

What to log
  • IDs — conversation, turn, user, session
  • Timestamps
  • The user’s question
  • Retrieved context (for RAG)
  • The model output
  • Latency, token counts, cost
  • Errors and behavioural signals
How logging must behave
  • Non-blocking — never slow the user down
  • Durable & queryable — analysis happens later
  • Late signal attachment — a thumbs-down arrives minutes after the answer; it must still find its turn
  • PII masked — logs are not a privacy leak

Tooling mentioned in the lecture: LangSmith for tracing and evaluation infrastructure.

6.6Closing the loop — the self-improving system

The two worlds feed each other. Online monitoring discovers real failures → those failures are added to the golden dataset → offline evals now cover them → fixes ship with confidence → repeat. The dataset grows forever, and the system gets harder to break with every incident.

Takeaway

Offline evals answer “is it correct?” before release; online evals answer “is it normal?” after. Logging is the spine, and production failures are the golden dataset’s food.

Model evals & capabilities

Evaluating the Model Itself

How to choose between models with evidence instead of hype — and the eight capabilities every benchmark map is drawn from.

7.1Why an AI engineer needs model evals

  • Choosing between models — GPT vs Claude vs Gemini vs open weights, for your workload.
  • Tracking new releases — every few weeks a new model claims the crown; evals tell you whether switching is worth it.
  • Safety posture — what will this model refuse, reveal, or confidently get wrong?
  • Build vs buy — is a frontier API necessary, or does a small self-hosted model clear your bar?

7.2The four-step process

  1. Decide the capability. Which of the eight capabilities actually matters for your product? A legal summariser lives and dies by long context, not by coding benchmarks.
  2. Bring a test. Pick a public benchmark for that capability — or build a custom eval set from your own data (better, when you can).
  3. Run every candidate under a fixed protocol. Same prompts, same settings, same scoring for every model — otherwise the comparison is fiction.
  4. Score and interpret. Numbers plus your constraints: cost, latency, privacy, hosting. The “best” model is the best one for the product.

7.3Benchmarks vs custom eval sets

7.5The eight capabilities, in detail

01 · Knowledge & reasoning

Does the model know things and can it reason over them?

MMLU — 57 subjects spanning humanities, STEM and social sciences.
02 · Coding & software eng.

The breakout LLM business — coding assistants have reached multi-billion-dollar valuations (Cursor & co.).

HumanEval — write functions from docstrings · SWE-bench — resolve real GitHub issues.
03 · Mathematics

Multi-step arithmetic and word problems — reasoning you can verify mechanically.

GSM8K — grade-school math word problems.
04 · Long context

A 128k-token window is marketing; can the model actually use it?

Needle-in-a-Haystack — hide one fact in a huge document, ask about it.
05 · Vision & multimodal

Reading charts, diagrams, screenshots and photos together with text.

MMMU — college-level questions mixing text and images.
06 · Agentic & tool use

The agent trilogy: pick the right tool, pass the right parameters, chain calls in the right order — and recover from errors.

Tool-calling suites — measured per Chapter 04’s agent risk rows.
07 · Safety & alignment

Truthfulness vs sycophancy (agreeing with the user instead of being right), refusal behaviour, and dangerous-capability checks such as cybersecurity benchmarks.

08 · Instruction following

Obeying verifiable constraints: “three bullet points”, “under 100 words”, “JSON only”.

IFEval — instructions with checkable compliance.
Takeaway

Capability first, test second, fixed protocol third, interpretation last. And when the numbers land, remember Model B: the right model is the one that clears your bar at a cost and speed your product can live with.
